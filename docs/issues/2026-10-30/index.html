Final election results: neither Netanyahu's bloc nor Eisenkot's reaches 61 seats

With every vote counted, Israel's election has produced a deadlock, and coalition talks may take months.

The Central Elections Committee published the final results early Friday. Turnout was 77.9 percent, which the Times of Israel describes as the highest in decades. Prime Minister Benjamin Netanyahu's bloc won 53 of the Knesset's 120 seats, down from 68 in the outgoing Knesset. Gadi Eisenkot's anti-Netanyahu bloc won 48. The Arab parties, the Joint List and Ra'am, won a record 19 between them. By party, Likud has 27 seats and Eisenkot's Yashar has 21.

A government needs 61 seats. Adding the Arab parties would give Eisenkot's side a majority on paper, but every Zionist party except The Democrats has ruled out working with them, so neither side is close. The official results go to President Isaac Herzog, who will ask each party leader whom they recommend as prime minister. The new Knesset is due to be sworn in on November 10, and coalition talks are expected to be difficult and could last more than two months. Herzog urged leaders to avoid another election, saying, "We must do everything we can, certainly after yesterday, to avoid another round of elections." Both Netanyahu and Eisenkot say they will form the next government.

Sources: Times of Israel, Ynet

llm-d 1.0: an open-source traffic controller for AI model serving

A group including Google Cloud, Red Hat, IBM, CoreWeave and NVIDIA released version 1.0 of a free system for running AI models across many servers.

What happened
llm-d 1.0 was announced on October 20 at the PyTorch Conference and is a CNCF sandbox project. It runs on Kubernetes and works with the vLLM and SGLang serving programs. Its features include routing requests by shared prompt prefix and server load, splitting the prefill and decode phases across machines, and offloading the KV cache to cheaper memory tiers. The 1.0 release adds multi-tenant traffic controls, high-availability routing and a stable configuration API. Google Cloud says it is integrating it into Google Kubernetes Engine and showed 1,000 sandboxed agents using one endpoint.
What it means, in plain words
Serving a chatbot at scale is like running a restaurant with many kitchens. Each kitchen (a GPU server) remembers the earlier conversation of the customers it has served. If a returning customer is sent to a kitchen that remembers them, the answer comes faster and cheaper. llm-d is the host at the door that decides which kitchen each request goes to, based on who remembers what and how busy each one is.
Why it matters and how it could be used
A company running its own model can use it to cut GPU cost, since reusing remembered work means the GPUs do less repeated computing. Reported figures include prefix routing capturing 96 to 97 percent of the available cache reuse and one team lifting its cache hit rate from about 30 to 75 percent. These come from the project and its users, not independent tests.

Jargon, explained

Kubernetes
A widely used system that starts, stops and manages programs running across many computers.
KV cache
The model's short-term memory of the text it has already read, which saves it from re-reading everything for each new word.
Prefill and decode
Prefill is the model reading your prompt; decode is it writing the answer one piece at a time.

NVIDIA releases Nemotron 3.5 Transcribe, an open speech model that does more than write down words

One downloadable model can transcribe 40 languages, label who said what, and follow plain-English instructions about how to write it out.

What happened
On October 28 NVIDIA released Nemotron 3.5 Transcribe with open weights under the OpenMDW-1.1 license. NVIDIA says it transcribes 40 languages, can also translate speech and identify the spoken language, labels speakers even when they talk over each other, and handles recordings up to one hour. It combines a speech encoder, NVIDIA's speaker-separation model and its Nemotron 3.5 Lightning language model, with about 4.3 billion parameters active per step. NVIDIA reports an average word error rate of 4.71 percent on the English Open ASR Leaderboard test sets and 4.62 percent on the multilingual one. Versions in BF16, FP8 and NVFP4 number formats are available.
What it means, in plain words
Older speech-to-text systems only turn sound into words. This one bolts an ear onto a language model, so you can give it instructions like 'write it as clean prose' or 'keep every um and uh' or 'mark who is speaking', and it does that in one pass. A 4.7 percent word error rate means roughly one word in twenty is wrong, on NVIDIA's own tests.
Why it matters and how it could be used
Because the weights are open, a company can run it on its own GPUs and keep recordings private, for example to transcribe and label speakers in an hour-long team meeting without sending audio to an outside service. It is also a good example of today's study topic: the audio is turned into tokens the language model reads, so prefill and decode apply here too. The accuracy figures are NVIDIA's own and not independently checked.

Jargon, explained

Open weights
The model's trained numbers are published, so anyone can download and run it on their own computers.
Word error rate
The share of words a transcription gets wrong. Lower is better.
Active parameters
Some models only use part of their numbers for each step. Active parameters are the part actually used, which sets how much computing each step costs.
FP8 and NVFP4
Smaller number formats that make a model faster and cheaper to run, at a small cost in precision.

Sources: NVIDIA Research, The Next Web

Why did ancient humans avoid this lush Arabian paradise?

Thousands of years ago the Arabian desert was green and full of lakes, yet an expedition found no sign that early people settled there.

The Rub' al Khali, or Empty Quarter, is among the driest places on Earth, but in past wetter periods it held lakes and rivers. You would expect early humans to move in wherever there was water. Nature reports on an expedition that looked for traces of ancient settlement and found none.

A companion research paper, titled "Tethered to stone: how rock and water availability shaped early desert occupation", concludes that water alone does not predict where early people lived. The idea is that people also needed workable stone to make tools, so a place with water but no good rock may have been unattractive. We could only read the summaries of these items, not the full text.

  1. What they found: no sign of ancient settlement in the Rub' al Khali, even when the region had water.
  2. How: an expedition surveyed the desert, and a companion paper looked at rock and water availability across early desert sites.
  3. Why it matters: it suggests early people chose where to live by more than water, including access to stone for tools.

What happens when you send a prompt: tokens, prefill and decode

Friday track: AI inference

The big idea: A language model doesn't read words; it reads numbered chunks called tokens. It first reads your whole prompt in one fast, parallel pass (prefill), then writes its answer one token at a time (decode), and those two phases stress the hardware in opposite ways.

First, the basics

A model is a huge pile of numbers (its weights) that turns a list of numbers into a prediction. Text has to be converted first. A tokenizer splits text into tokens, which are pieces of words such as 'net', 'work' or ' the', and each token has an ID in a fixed vocabulary, usually tens of thousands to a couple of hundred thousand entries. A rough rule of thumb is that one token is about three quarters of an English word.

The model's one job is this: given all the tokens so far, produce a probability for every possible next token, then pick one. That's all. A whole answer is produced by repeating this step, feeding each new token back in as input. This repeated loop is called autoregressive generation.

The hardware bottleneck matters here. A GPU can do an enormous amount of math per second, but every calculation needs weights fetched from memory. Whether the GPU is limited by math or by fetching from memory decides how fast each phase runs.

How it works, step by step
  1. Your text is split by the tokenizer into token IDs, so 'Explain RDMA' might become a short list of numbers.
  2. Each ID is turned into a vector (a list of numbers) by an embedding table, which is the form the model can calculate with.
  3. Prefill: the model processes all prompt tokens at once, through every layer. Because the tokens are processed together, the GPU's math units stay busy, so this phase is limited by compute.
  4. During prefill, each layer saves intermediate results for every prompt token (the keys and values, the KV cache) so they never need to be recomputed.
  5. The model outputs probabilities for the next token, and a sampling rule picks one. This first token is what the user waits for, so the delay before it appears is called time to first token.
  6. Decode: the new token is fed back in, but now only one token is processed per step, while the full set of weights still has to be read from memory. This phase is limited by memory bandwidth, not math.
  7. The loop repeats, adding one token per step to the KV cache, until the model produces an end-of-answer token or hits a length limit.
A practical example
You paste a 3,000-token document into a chatbot running an 8-billion-parameter model on one H100 GPU and ask for a summary. Prefill reads all 3,000 tokens in one burst, then the answer starts to stream. If the summary is 300 tokens, decode runs 300 separate steps, and each one has to read the model's roughly 16 GB of weights from memory. At about 3.35 TB per second that is about 5 ms per step at best, so the 300-token answer takes at least about a second and a half however fast the math units are.
Why it matters for XLIO
Serving systems split these two phases across different machines and move the KV cache between them over the network. That transfer is a latency-sensitive job for fast networking, which is the kind of problem NVIDIA's networking stack and XLIO-style user-space I/O are meant to speed up.
Key numbers
Rule of thumb for Englishabout 0.75 words per token
Weights of an 8-billion-parameter model at 16 bits (2 bytes each)about 16 GB
H100 SXM GPU memory bandwidthabout 3.35 TB per second
Best case per decode step for one request on that GPU (16 GB / 3.35 TB/s)about 5 ms, so at most about 200 tokens per second
Common misconception
A common misconception is that the model 'thinks' about the whole answer first and then types it out. In fact it only ever decides one token at a time, and a streaming answer is generated live, token by token.

Jargon, explained

Token
A small chunk of text, often part of a word, that the model treats as one unit.
Prefill
The first phase, where the model reads the whole prompt at once.
Decode
The second phase, where the model produces the answer one token at a time.
KV cache
Saved intermediate results for the tokens so far, so they aren't recomputed at every step.
Time to first token
How long you wait before the first piece of the answer appears.
Self-test: Why is decode usually limited by memory speed rather than by how fast the GPU can do math?

Each decode step handles only one new token, so there's very little math per step, but the GPU still has to read all the model's weights from memory to do it. The math units spend most of the time waiting for data.
