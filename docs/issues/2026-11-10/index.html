Eisenkot gets the mandate to form Israel's next government, but the numbers are hard

President Herzog asked Yashar leader Gadi Eisenkot to try to build a coalition, and as the new Knesset is sworn in today, nobody yet has a clear path to 61 seats.

On November 8, President Isaac Herzog gave Gadi Eisenkot, leader of the Yashar party, the job of forming the next government, according to the Times of Israel. Sixty-seven of the Knesset's 120 members had recommended him: a Jewish-led bloc of 48 and an Arab-led bloc of 19. Benjamin Netanyahu is recommended by 46, and Likud is the largest single party with 27 seats. A government needs 61 votes to win a confidence vote. Eisenkot said he wants a national emergency government and that it will take what he called creative and unconventional political acts. He listed four principles for any partner: Israel as a Jewish and democratic state, the values of the Declaration of Independence, mandatory national service for all, and recognizing Hamas as a terror group and committing to its destruction.

The obstacle is arithmetic. According to the report, Arab parties have said they will back an Eisenkot government or at least not vote against it, while some members of his Jewish bloc say they will not join a government that depends on those votes. Reported options include a minority government or bringing in the Haredi (ultra-Orthodox) parties, long allies of Netanyahu. Times of Israel reports that Avigdor Liberman of Yisrael Beytenu has cleared Eisenkot to approach the Haredim and that the two agreed on a Knesset speaker, with Eisenkot possibly taking that role while he builds a government. The clock: Eisenkot has up to 42 days. If he fails, Netanyahu gets 28 days, and if neither succeeds, 61 members can still agree on a third candidate within 21 days, after which the process leads to new elections. Eisenkot rejects that outcome. The 26th Knesset, with 46 first-time members, is sworn in today.

Sources: Times of Israel, Times of Israel

OpenAI says its AI agents found 948 ways to take over Linux machines, then helped patch them

An automated pipeline hunted for kernel bugs, wrote working exploits, and proposed fixes, at a claimed cost of about $1.2 million.

What happened
In a November 4 report, OpenAI says a prototype pipeline of AI agents analyzed 2,308 candidate flaws in the Linux kernel, reproduced 1,724 of them, and produced exploits for 948 distinct bugs that let an ordinary user become administrator. The run used $1,213,430 in API credits, which OpenAI calls an upper bound. Human researchers review each finding before it is reported to maintainers. OpenAI says that while the campaign was running 61 of the bugs were already patched upstream and 43 had staged patches.
What it means, in plain words
The kernel is the core of the operating system, and a bug that lets any user gain full control is called a privilege escalation. Finding these used to need rare human experts. OpenAI is showing that agents can do the whole loop: find the bug, prove it is real by exploiting it, and write the fix. OpenAI also says that in an earlier run, 20 of 200 apparent successes actually exploited a different bug than assigned, so each exploit is now checked against its intended flaw.
Why it matters and how it could be used
Attackers can use the same techniques, so defenders get patches faster only if maintainers can keep up with the volume. For someone working near kernels and drivers, such as network stacks, it means bugs in that code will be found at machine speed. Note that OpenAI is keeping some technique details back, and these are the company's own figures.

Jargon, explained

Privilege escalation
A bug that lets a normal user gain full administrator control of a computer.
Exploit
A program that takes advantage of a bug to make it do something harmful.
Kernel
The core of the operating system that manages hardware and memory for every program.

MIT researchers let an AI model edit another model's weights directly

Instead of retraining, a frontier model read a small network's numbers and rewrote some of them, and one small model's math score jumped from 7.9% to 48.1%.

What happened
A paper-style blog post from MIT CSAIL (Takehi, Teoh, Zhang and Khattab) describes weight-space programming: a general-purpose model reads a network's raw weights as text and edits or writes them from a plain-language goal. The authors anonymize the model as GPT-6 Astra. Editing 131,072 weights (0.016%) of Qwen3.5-0.8B to stop endless reasoning loops raised MATH-500 from 7.9% to 48.1% and GSM8K from 13.8% to 67.1%, and answers hitting the token limit fell from 93% to 33%. In a driving game, one edit changed 3.5% of the weights and succeeded in all 1,024 episodes, where the conventionally trained policy never succeeded in 256.
What it means, in plain words
A neural network is just a huge table of numbers called weights. Normally you adjust them slowly through training. Here a smart model reads the table like a program, finds what is causing a failure, and changes a few entries by hand, like a mechanic opening the engine instead of rebuilding the car. The Qwen fix worked because that small model often got stuck looping, so removing the loop unlocked ability it already had.
Why it matters and how it could be used
If it scales, models could be debugged and customized in minutes without training runs. The authors are clear about limits: it gave no gain on Gemma 3 270M, which rarely loops, so editing removes obstacles but cannot add missing skills. Most experiments are small, results depend on tools and compute budgets, and the work is early and self-reported.

Jargon, explained

Weights
The numbers inside a neural network that store everything it has learned.
MATH-500
A set of 500 math problems used to score models.
Gradient descent
The usual training method that nudges weights a little at a time to reduce errors.

Moonshot releases Kimi K3.1 Pro, a 2.8-trillion-parameter model with open weights

A huge but efficient model that only uses about 104 billion parameters per word, with a one-million-token context.

What happened
Moonshot AI published Kimi K3.1 Pro on Hugging Face under its own custom license. The model card says it has 2.8 trillion total parameters with about 104 billion active per token, uses a mixture-of-experts design with 896 experts (16 picked per token), accepts text and images, and handles about 1 million tokens of context. It is trained with 4-bit weights. Moonshot reports scores such as 94.2 on GPQA-Diamond and 79.6 on OSWorld V2.1; these are Moonshot's own claims, and some comparison numbers come from other vendors' reports.
What it means, in plain words
Mixture-of-experts means the model is like a hospital with 896 specialists: for each word it calls only 16 of them (plus 2 always-on), so it runs far cheaper than its total size suggests. Open weights means you can download the numbers and run it yourself, though this license has custom terms.
Why it matters and how it could be used
For inference engineers, such a model still needs large multi-GPU systems and fast networking between them, since experts live on different GPUs. Moonshot recommends the serving engines vLLM and SGLang. It also shows open models staying close to the proprietary frontier on agent and coding tests, by the vendor's own measure.

Context window
How much text the model can consider at once; 1 million tokens is roughly several long books.

inferd: an open-source engine built to run many AI agents at once on a Mac

It applies data-center serving tricks, such as continuous batching and speculative decoding, to Apple Silicon.

What happened
Incept5 released inferd, an experimental Apache-2.0 inference engine for Apple Silicon. It serves one model to many concurrent agents using continuous batching, speculative decoding, an 8-bit KV cache stored in pages, a prefix cache that shares one agent's context with others, and an SSD tier for contexts that don't fit in memory. The project reports 849 tokens per second for 64 agents on 16k contexts on a Mac Studio M5 Ultra with a 27B model. These are the authors' own benchmarks, and they say other engines are faster for a single stream.
What it means, in plain words
When an AI agent works, it keeps a large memory of the conversation so far, called the KV cache. With many agents, those memories are the bottleneck. inferd saves common starting contexts once and lets many agents reuse them, and parks idle ones on disk, like keeping files on a shelf instead of the desk.
Why it matters and how it could be used
It shows that techniques from big inference servers are moving down to desktop machines, so a small team could run a fleet of coding agents locally. It is young software with no independent reviews yet.

Jargon, explained

Continuous batching
Serving many requests together and letting new ones join as others finish.
Speculative decoding
A small model guesses several tokens ahead and the big model checks them in one go.
KV cache
The stored intermediate results for earlier tokens so they are not recomputed.

Sources: inferd on GitHub

Sequence numbers, ACKs and retransmission: how TCP knows what arrived

Tuesday track: TCP in depth

The big idea: TCP numbers every byte it sends, the receiver keeps telling the sender the next byte number it expects, and if that answer stops advancing the sender sends the missing bytes again.

First, the basics

The network underneath TCP, IP, is a best-effort service. Packets can be lost, duplicated, delayed or delivered out of order. TCP's job is to hide that and give the application a clean stream of bytes. To do that it needs a way to say which bytes arrived.

TCP cuts your data into pieces called segments. The largest chunk of application data in one segment is the MSS, typically 1460 bytes on Ethernet (a 1500-byte packet minus 40 bytes of IP and TCP headers). Think of mailing a book in numbered envelopes: if the recipient says 'I have everything up to page 300', you know exactly what to resend.

How it works, step by step
  1. At connection setup each side picks a random starting sequence number (the ISN). The SYN flag itself uses up one number.
  2. Every byte of data has a sequence number. A segment carries the number of its first byte in the header, a 32-bit field. A segment with 1460 bytes starting at 1000 covers bytes 1000 to 2459.
  3. The receiver replies with an ACK containing the next byte it expects, here 2460. ACKs are cumulative: 'everything before 2460 is safe'. The receiver usually acknowledges every second full segment, or after a short delay, to save packets.
  4. The sender keeps a copy of unacknowledged data and starts a retransmission timer (RTO) for it. If the ACK number moves past the data, the copy is freed and the timer is reset or stopped.
  5. If the timer expires first, the sender retransmits the oldest unacknowledged segment and doubles the timer (exponential backoff), so a congested network is not flooded with retries.
  6. If a segment arrives out of order, the receiver holds it and repeats the same ACK number. The sender notices repeated identical ACKs, which lead to faster recovery methods covered in a later lesson.
A practical example
A client sends 3 segments starting at byte 1000, and the second is lost. The server receives bytes 1000 to 2459 and ACKs 2460. It then gets the third segment, which starts at 3920, but it cannot ACK past the gap, so it ACKs 2460 again. The client sees ACK 2460 stuck while the data it sent goes beyond, and eventually it resends the segment starting at 2460. Once that arrives the server ACKs 5380 in one go, covering all three segments.
Why it matters for XLIO
A user-space TCP stack like XLIO has to implement all of this itself: tracking sequence numbers per connection, holding unacknowledged data until it is ACKed, and running retransmission timers, work the kernel normally does. Because zero-copy sends keep user buffers in place until ACKed, the retransmission state also decides when an application's buffer can be reused.

Common misconception
A common misconception is that sequence numbers count packets. They count bytes, which is why the ACK number tells you exactly how much data arrived, regardless of how it was split into segments. Another is that an ACK means the application read the data; it only means the receiver's TCP stack has it.

Jargon, explained

Segment
One TCP packet's worth of data plus its header.
ACK
Acknowledgment: a message telling the sender the next byte number the receiver expects.
RTT
Round-trip time: how long a message takes to go to the other side and the answer to come back.
RTO
Retransmission timeout: how long the sender waits for an ACK before resending.
Exponential backoff
Doubling the wait after each failed attempt.
Cumulative ACK
An ACK that confirms all bytes before a given number.
Self-test: Why does TCP not measure the round-trip time using a segment that was retransmitted?

Because when an ACK arrives you cannot tell whether it answers the original send or the retransmission, so the measured time could be badly wrong. TCP's rule (Karn's algorithm) is to skip such samples for timing, and it keeps the backed-off timer until a fresh segment is acknowledged. Recap: TCP numbers bytes, the receiver says which byte comes next, and a stuck ACK or an expired timer triggers a resend.

Further reading: RFC 9293: Transmission Control Protocol, RFC 6298: Computing TCP's Retransmission Timer
