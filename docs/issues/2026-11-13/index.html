Google launches Gemini 4 Hydrogen, a fast model it says matches its frontier model on a key index

A cheaper, quicker everyday model built for AI agents, now powering Google Search.

What happened
On November 12, Google released Gemini 4 Hydrogen, which it calls its "daily driver for agentic workflows." Google says it scores 51 on the Artificial Analysis Intelligence Index at its default setting, versus 50 for its bigger frontier model Gemini 4 Argon, and 54 at its highest thinking level. Google says it finishes tasks 2.5 times faster than the older Gemini 3.8 Flash and scores 57% on the Terminal-Bench 4.1 coding benchmark versus 18.2%. It costs $1.50 per million input tokens and $7.50 per million output tokens, accepts up to 1 million tokens of input, and has four thinking levels: low, medium, high and xhigh. It is rolling out in the Gemini API, AI Studio, the Gemini app and Google Search.
What it means, in plain words
AI companies usually sell a family: a big, slow, expensive model for hard problems and a small, fast, cheap one for everyday work. Google is claiming its fast model has now caught up with its big one on a broad intelligence score. "Thinking levels" let you choose how long the model reasons before it answers, like telling a colleague "quick answer" or "take your time." An agent is a model that works in a loop, taking many small actions such as running a command or searching a file, so speed per step adds up fast.
Why it matters and how it could be used
For agents, the cost and delay of each step matter more than peak brilliance, because a task can take hundreds of steps. If the claims hold up, developers can run coding or data agents on a cheaper model without losing much quality. These are Google's own benchmarks; independent tests will show whether the gap with bigger models is really closed.

NVIDIA open-sources Snapshot, which restarts AI inference servers in seconds instead of minutes

Instead of booting a model server from scratch, you save it once fully loaded and copy that saved state onto new GPUs.

What happened
In a November 10 technical blog post, NVIDIA released Snapshot, an open-source checkpoint-and-restore tool for GPU workloads on Kubernetes, first built for its Dynamo inference software. It saves a fully started model server, including both CPU and GPU memory, and starts new copies from that saved state. NVIDIA says restores took 3.6 to 42.7 seconds on B200 GPUs, 8 to 36 times faster than cold starts, which took 6 to 19 minutes for the largest mixture-of-experts models, with checkpoints up to about 1 TB across eight GPUs. It works with vLLM, SGLang and TensorRT-LLM. NVIDIA says design partner Crusoe cut startup for one model from about 8 minutes to 31 seconds.
What it means, in plain words
Starting an AI model server is slow: it loads hundreds of gigabytes of weights into GPU memory, compiles code for the GPU and warms up. Snapshot works like hibernating a laptop: you freeze the server once it is fully ready, save everything to storage, and later wake up identical copies on other machines. It uses CRIU, an existing Linux tool that freezes a running program and saves it to disk, plus a step that copies the GPU's memory back.
Why it matters and how it could be used
When traffic spikes, an AI service needs more copies of its model fast. If each copy takes 10 minutes to start, companies keep expensive GPUs idling just in case; if it takes 30 seconds, they can scale up on demand and save money. It also shows where NVIDIA is putting effort: not only faster chips, but software that keeps expensive GPUs busy.

Jargon, explained

Inference
Running a trained model to answer requests, as opposed to training it.
Cold start
Starting a program from nothing, loading and initializing everything, which is slow.
Kubernetes
Software that runs and manages many copies of programs across a cluster of machines.
Checkpoint and restore
Saving the complete state of a running program so it can be resumed later, possibly on another machine.

Found: a protein that drives ageing in organs without the usual inflammation

Old mice born without one immune protein, granzyme K, had healthier hearts, livers and kidneys, even though their immune systems aged normally.

As we age, our immune cells make more of a protein called granzyme K. Immune cells use proteins like this as tiny scissors, cutting up other proteins. A team at Monash University in Australia, led by Professor Nicole La Gruta, wanted to know whether this rising protein actually damages our organs or is just a sign of getting older. They compared old mice bred without granzyme K to normal old mice.

The mice without it had better heart function and less scarring in the heart, and fewer signs of age damage in the liver and kidneys. Surprisingly, their immune systems still aged as usual, which suggests organ ageing and immune ageing can be pulled apart, against a long-held assumption that they go together. In human data, granzyme K levels were high in patients with several inflammatory and scarring diseases, including heart failure and a kidney complication of lupus. The study was published in the journal Immunity; Nature's news team covered it.

There are big caveats. This was in mice, and the mice lacked the protein their whole lives, so nobody yet knows whether blocking it later in life, as a drug would, would help. The team is now looking for ways to block granzyme K selectively.

  1. What they found: old mice without granzyme K had healthier hearts, livers and kidneys, while their immune systems aged normally.
  2. How: they compared old mice genetically engineered to lack the protein with normal old mice, and checked human patient data for the protein.
  3. Why it matters: it points to a single, specific target that might one day be blocked to slow organ ageing, without weakening the immune system.

Jargon, explained

Granzyme K
A protein made by some immune cells that cuts other proteins; its levels rise with age.
Fibrosis

Knockout mice
Mice bred so that a particular gene, and so its protein, is missing.

Batching and continuous batching: serving many users at once

Friday track: AI inference

The big idea: Generating each new token forces the GPU to read the entire model from memory, and that read costs about the same whether it serves one user or a hundred, so servers process many users' requests together in one batch. Continuous batching goes further: it reshuffles the batch after every single token, so a finished request leaves immediately and a waiting one takes its seat.

First, the basics

Recall from earlier Fridays: answering a prompt has two phases. Prefill reads your whole prompt at once and fills the KV cache, the model's notes about every token so far. Decode then produces the answer one token at a time, and every token requires a full pass through the model.

A GPU has huge compute power but limited memory bandwidth, the speed at which it can pull data out of its memory. During decode with a single user, each token needs every weight of the model to be read once but does only a little math with each weight. For a 7 billion parameter model stored in 16-bit numbers, that is about 14 GB to read per token; an H100 reads roughly 3.35 TB per second, so one user tops out around 240 tokens per second while the math units mostly sit idle.

Batching fixes this: if 64 users each need their next token, the GPU reads each weight once and uses it 64 times. The memory read is shared, so the batch finishes in not much more time than a single request. The limit becomes memory to hold everyone's KV cache, not the weight reads.

How it works, step by step
  1. Requests arrive at different times with different prompt lengths and wildly different answer lengths, and wait in a queue.
  2. Static batching, the old way: the server groups, say, 8 requests, runs them together, and only starts the next group when all 8 are done. A request that finishes after 20 tokens leaves its seat empty while another runs to 2,000.
  3. Continuous batching, introduced by the Orca paper in 2022 as iteration-level scheduling: the scheduler decides who is in the batch at every step. Each step, it runs one decode token for every active request.
  4. When a request emits its end-of-text token or hits its length limit, it leaves the batch and its KV-cache memory is freed right away.
  5. The scheduler admits waiting requests into the freed space. Their prefill can be split into chunks (chunked prefill) and mixed into the same step as other users' decode tokens, so long prompts don't freeze everyone else.
  6. Limits keep it safe: in vLLM, max_num_seqs caps how many requests run at once and max_num_batched_tokens caps how many tokens are processed per step. vLLM runs pending decodes first, then fills leftover budget with prefill.
  7. If KV-cache memory runs out, the server preempts a request: it pauses it, frees its memory, and later recomputes its cache from scratch (vLLM's default) or swaps it out.
A practical example
Imagine an inference server answering 200 chat users. One asks for a one-line yes or no, another asks for a 1,500-word essay. With static batching, the yes-or-no user's seat sits empty for the entire essay. With continuous batching, that seat is handed to the next user in the queue on the very next step. In Anyscale's 2023 benchmark of a 13 billion parameter model on one A100, continuous batching alone gave about 8 times the throughput of naive static batching, and about 23 times when combined with vLLM's memory management.
Why it matters for XLIO
It's the same trick XLIO and NICs use: amortize a fixed per-operation cost over many items. A NIC polls one completion queue for many connections and rings one doorbell for several packets; an inference server reads the weights once for many users. And the network matters here too: with continuous batching, requests stream in and tokens stream out to hundreds of users at once, so the serving front end is a high-rate, many-small-messages network workload, exactly XLIO's territory.

Common misconception
Misconception: bigger batches always mean slower answers for each user. At small and medium batch sizes, decode is limited by reading the weights, so adding users costs each of them very little time per token; throughput rises almost for free. The real tradeoff appears later: once memory or compute fills up, each extra user slows everyone's tokens a bit, and big prefills mixed into the batch can cause stalls. That's why servers cap batch size and token budget per step.

Jargon, explained

Batch
A group of requests processed together in one pass on the GPU.
Memory bandwidth
How fast data can be read from memory, in bytes per second.
Throughput
Total work done per second, here tokens generated per second across all users.
Iteration-level scheduling
Re-deciding which requests are in the batch at every generation step, the core of continuous batching.
Chunked prefill
Splitting a long prompt's prefill into pieces so it can share steps with other users' decoding.
Preemption
Pausing a running request to free memory for others, and resuming it later.
Self-test: A server uses static batches of 8. Seven requests need 50 tokens each and one needs 1,000. Roughly what fraction of the seat-steps does useful work, and what does continuous batching change?

The batch runs 1,000 steps with 8 seats, so 8,000 seat-steps. Useful work is 7 x 50 + 1,000 = 1,350, about 17%. Over 80% of the seats sit empty after step 50. Continuous batching refills each seat as soon as its request ends, so seats stay busy as long as the queue has requests waiting.
