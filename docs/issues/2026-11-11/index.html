Netanyahu's opening Knesset speech derails coalition talks as the new majority moves to take the speaker's chair

After Netanyahu called Arab lawmakers supporters of terror and demanded a unity government, Eisenkot's Yashar party canceled talks with Likud, and 67 lawmakers asked for a vote to replace the Knesset speaker.

On Tuesday, right after the 26th Knesset was sworn in, caretaker Prime Minister Benjamin Netanyahu used his speech to demand a national unity government, according to the Times of Israel. He said the only permanent answer to Iran's nuclear program is toppling its regime, and that it is impossible to handle such threats with a narrow government. He called Arab lawmakers terror supporters and haters of Israel, said he was not talking about Arabs in general, and said Ra'am and the Joint List both want the Jewish state destroyed. He also urged the Haredi (ultra-Orthodox) parties not to back what he called a narrow government led by Gadi Eisenkot. Opposition members heckled him, and the Democrats party walked out.

Eisenkot, who holds the mandate to form a government, called the speech delusional, divisive and hate-filled, and his Yashar party canceled talks with Likud planned for Wednesday. Ra'am leader Mansour Abbas said Ra'am supports two states and condemns terrorism, and called the remarks a blood libel; the Joint List called the speech racist. Separately, Ynet reports that 67 lawmakers from the anti-Netanyahu bloc formally asked to elect a new speaker. Speaker Amir Ohana of Likud criticized the move but said he will respect the majority and set the vote for next Tuesday. The bloc's candidate is Yashar's Matan Kahana. A speaker controls the Knesset's agenda, so the vote is the first test of whether the 67-seat bloc can turn its numbers into control. The Haredi party UTJ says it will not vote to replace the speaker.

House shelves the bid to impeach Defense Secretary Hegseth, for now

On Tuesday the House voted 211 to 204 to send a resolution impeaching Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth back to committee, which stops it for now, AP reports via the Times of Israel. Republican Thomas Massie filed it with Democratic backing. Most of its eight articles concern the war with Iran, which Massie says has run 256 days without approval from Congress. Others cite cuts to a Pentagon office that tracks harm to civilians and strikes on alleged drug boats.

Hegseth called the effort sabotage and said the issues are policy disagreements, not crimes. Trump praised Hegseth and called the vote an exoneration, and Speaker Mike Johnson called it a political gambit. Incoming Speaker Hakeem Jeffries said Hegseth has abused his power. Democrats take control of both the House and Senate in January, so the effort may return.

Why it matters: It is the first test of how Congress will handle the Iran war after the midterms. Once Democrats hold the House in January, they can hold hearings and revisit impeachment, though removal needs two thirds of the Senate.

Anthropic releases Claude Fable 5.2 and Mythos 5.2, its top models for very long tasks

Anthropic says the new models are faster and stronger at coding and science, and that one coordinated over 1,000 helper agents for eight days to build a working operating system.

What happened
On November 9 Anthropic released Claude Fable 5.2 and Claude Mythos 5.2, which it says are the same underlying model with different access. Fable 5.2 is available to everyone at $8 per million input tokens and $40 per million output tokens, with heavy safeguards on cybersecurity and biology. Mythos 5.2 is limited to vetted organizations. Anthropic says responses are up to 30% faster than version 5.1. On its reported benchmarks, Fable 5.2 scores 69.9% on the FrontierSWE v2 coding test versus 62.3% for its everyday model Opus 5.5. In a demo, Anthropic says an engineer started with an empty folder and Fable 5.2 directed more than 1,000 subagents for eight days to build an operating system that boots on a laptop, with disk and network drivers, a file system and a login screen.
What it means, in plain words
Anthropic, like other labs, sells several models: cheaper everyday ones and more expensive ones for the hardest problems. Fable is the top tier. The headline idea is orchestration: one model acts like a project manager, splits a huge job into small tasks, hands them to many copies of itself, checks their work and stitches it together. An operating system is a good stress test because thousands of pieces must fit exactly. The same model is offered in two versions because its hacking skill is useful for defenders but dangerous in the wrong hands.
Why it matters and how it could be used
It shows AI moving from answering questions to running multi-day engineering projects. For someone writing systems code, a plausible use is handing an agent team a well-defined chunk, such as a test suite for a protocol parser, and reviewing its output. These are Anthropic's own numbers, and the operating system is a demo, not a product.

Jargon, explained

Token
A chunk of text, roughly three quarters of a word, that models read and write and that pricing is counted in.
Subagent
A copy of a model given one small task by a coordinating model, like a team member taking one ticket.
Benchmark
A fixed test set used to compare models with a score.

Google adds two new AI chips, including one built to keep data secret while it is processed

The TPU 8tx targets governments and banks with private data, and the TPU 8i Max packs two chips together for memory-hungry AI agents.

What happened
On November 9 Google announced two more chips in its eighth generation of TPUs, its own alternative to NVIDIA GPUs, bringing the generation to four systems. The TPU 8tx uses the same silicon as its TPU 8t training chip and adds hardware protection that keeps data and model weights isolated while they are being used; Google says its network design cuts latency by up to 50% versus the 8t. The TPU 8i Max combines two TPU 8i inference chips in one package, doubling compute, on-chip memory and memory bandwidth. Google's Amin Vahdat wrote that the TPU business now has nearly 100 customers. Prices and dates were not given.
What it means, in plain words
Normally data is encrypted when stored or sent, but has to be decrypted to be computed on, which is the moment it is exposed. Confidential computing closes that gap by making the chip itself wall off the data, even from the cloud operator. The 8i Max addresses a different problem: when an agent reads a whole codebase or hours of video, the chip spends most of its time waiting for data from memory, so more memory bandwidth helps more than more raw math.
Why it matters and how it could be used
It shows the AI chip race splitting into specialized products, and that memory bandwidth, not just compute, is becoming the selling point for inference. A hospital or bank that could not send patient or customer data to a shared cloud could, in principle, fine-tune a model on it with hardware guarantees. These are Google's claims; independent tests are not yet available.

Jargon, explained

TPU
Tensor Processing Unit: Google's own chip designed for AI math.
Confidential computing
Hardware that keeps data isolated and protected even while it is being processed.
Memory bandwidth
How many bytes per second a chip can pull from its memory; often the real speed limit for AI.

Lenders are dumping loans backed by AI chips, worried the GPUs age too fast

Banks and funds that lent money for data centers are trying to sell those loans at steep discounts, Semafor reports.

What happened
Semafor reports that whole portfolios of data-center loans are being shopped at steep discounts, and that loans backed by GPUs are hit hardest; one source said they are getting smoked. What it means, in plain words
Many AI data centers are paid for with borrowed money, and the GPUs themselves serve as the collateral, like a car backing a car loan. The worry is that a new GPU generation arrives roughly every year, so a chip may lose most of its value before a five-year loan is paid off. If the collateral is worth less than the debt, lenders lose money when a borrower fails.
Why it matters and how it could be used
The AI building boom depends on cheap, plentiful credit. If lenders pull back, data-center projects become harder or more expensive to fund, which could slow the build-out that NVIDIA and its networking business rely on. The $40 billion SpaceX deal will be a test of investor appetite.

Meta launches Muse Ember, a very cheap model for the grunt work inside AI agents

Meta says its smallest model nearly matches a bigger sibling on a broad test at about a seventeenth of the cost per task.

What happened
On November 9 Meta released Muse Ember, its smallest and most efficient model, aimed at agent subtasks such as tool calls, reading files, running tests and pulling data out of documents. It costs $0.10 per million input tokens and $0.50 per million output tokens, has a 1 million token context window, and accepts text, images, audio and video. Meta says it scores 36 on the Artificial Analysis Intelligence Index versus 37 for its larger Muse Spark 1.3, at about $0.14 per test task versus $2.39.
What it means, in plain words
An agent doing real work makes hundreds of small model calls, most of them simple: open this file, extract that field, decide which tool to use. Paying top-model prices for each of those is like having a senior engineer do data entry. A small model handles the routine steps cheaply, and Meta suggests calling its biggest model, Muse Wonder, only for the hard parts.
Why it matters and how it could be used
Cost per step decides whether agents are affordable at scale. A practical pattern is routing: send routine calls to a cheap model and escalate only hard ones. These are Meta's benchmark figures, measured against its own chosen comparisons.

A toothbrush that makes its own electricity to fight plaque

Bristles made of a special plastic generate tiny charges as they bend, and in a three-month trial they cut plaque far more than ordinary brushes.

Nature's news team covered a study from Peking University's School of Stomatology that redesigns the humble manual toothbrush. Ordinary bristles are nylon. These are made of PVDF, a plastic that produces a small electrical charge whenever it is bent, the way some lighters make a spark when you press them. Brushing bends the bristles thousands of times, so the brush powers itself, with no battery.

According to the study, published in Science Advances, the charges react with water in the mouth to make substances that harm bacteria but not human cells. In a trial with 115 adults over three months, plaque scores fell about 40% for people using the new brushes, compared with about 15% for people using standard nylon brushes. The new-brush users also had less gum bleeding and inflammation and fewer disease-linked bacteria. The authors say longer follow-up is still needed, so durability and long-term benefit are not yet known.

  1. Found: brushes with self-charging bristles cut plaque by about 40% in three months, versus about 15% for ordinary brushes.
  2. How: 115 adults brushed twice a day with fluoride-free toothpaste, and dentists scored their plaque and sequenced the bacteria in it.
  3. Why it matters: a cheap, battery-free change to an everyday object could reduce gum disease, but longer and larger studies are needed.

Jargon, explained

Piezoelectric
A material that makes electricity when squeezed or bent.
Plaque
The sticky film of bacteria that builds up on teeth and causes decay and gum disease.

The memory hierarchy: registers, caches, DRAM, and why distance costs time

Wednesday track: computer architecture and performance

The big idea: A CPU can compute far faster than main memory can feed it, so computers keep a few small, fast copies of recently used data close to the core; a fast program is mostly one that finds its data in those close copies.

First, the basics

A modern core runs at about 3 GHz, so one clock cycle lasts about a third of a nanosecond. In that time light travels about 10 centimeters, and electrical signals on a chip move slower still. Memory that is physically farther away, larger, and built from slower cells simply cannot answer in one cycle.

There are two kinds of memory cells. SRAM, used in caches, stores each bit in a small circuit of about six transistors: fast but big and expensive. DRAM, used for main memory, stores each bit as charge in a tiny capacitor: dense and cheap, but slower, and it must be refreshed constantly. You cannot have memory that is huge, cheap and fast at once, so you build layers.

Programs help by having locality. Temporal locality means data used now is likely used again soon, like a loop counter. Spatial locality means data next to it is likely used next, like the next element of an array. Caches exploit both.

How it works, step by step
  1. The core works on registers, a handful of named slots inside the core itself. Reading one costs effectively nothing. Everything else is a load from memory.
  2. On a load, the core first checks the L1 data cache, private to that core. On Intel's Golden Cove cores it is 48 KB and answers in about 5 cycles.
  3. On an L1 miss it checks L2, also private: about 2 MB per core on Sapphire Rapids servers, about 16 cycles.
  4. On an L2 miss it checks the L3, shared by all cores on the chip and tens of megabytes. Because it sits across an on-chip network, it takes around 33 nanoseconds on Sapphire Rapids, roughly 100 to 130 cycles.
  5. On an L3 miss the request goes to the memory controller and out to DRAM, around 100 nanoseconds or about 300 cycles. During that time a core could have executed hundreds of instructions.
  6. Data never moves as a single byte. Every level moves whole cache lines of 64 bytes, so reading one byte brings its 63 neighbors along for free, which is why scanning an array in order is fast.
  7. When a line comes back it is copied into the caches on its way in, evicting some older line. Hardware prefetchers watch the access pattern and fetch the next lines early, hiding latency for regular patterns but not for random pointer-chasing.
A practical example
Imagine a receive loop that touches a 64-byte completion entry, then the packet buffer it points to, then a per-connection struct found through a hash table. If all three are in L1 or L2, the whole step costs a few nanoseconds. If the connection struct was evicted and must come from DRAM, that one miss costs about 100 nanoseconds, more than the rest of the work combined. At 10 million packets per second you have only 100 nanoseconds per packet in total, so a single DRAM miss per packet uses up the whole budget.
Why it matters for XLIO
XLIO's speed depends on keeping its hot data, like ring and completion structures and per-socket state, in cache. Polling the completion queue from the same core that handles the socket keeps those lines warm. Intel CPUs also have a feature called DDIO that lets the NIC's DMA writes land directly in a slice of the L3 cache instead of DRAM, so a freshly arrived packet can be read from L3 rather than main memory.

Common misconception
A common belief is that more RAM makes a program faster. Capacity only matters if you run out; what limits most hot loops is latency, how long each miss takes. A program whose working set fits in L2 can be several times faster than the same program whose data is scattered across gigabytes, even on a machine with plenty of free memory.

Jargon, explained

Cache
A small, fast memory that keeps copies of recently used data close to the core.
Cache line
The fixed 64-byte block that caches load and store as a unit.
Cache miss
When the data is not in a cache level and must be fetched from a slower, farther level.
DRAM
The main memory chips, dense and cheap but much slower than caches.
Prefetcher
Hardware that guesses which data you will need next and fetches it early.
Locality
The tendency of programs to reuse the same data or nearby data soon.
Self-test: A program sums an array of 16 million 4-byte integers, first in order and then in a random order. Why is the random version several times slower even though it does the same additions?

In order, each 64-byte line brings 16 integers, so only one in 16 loads can miss, and the prefetcher fetches the next lines ahead of time. In random order, almost every load lands on a different line that is not in any cache (the 64 MB array is larger than L3), so most loads pay the full trip to DRAM, around 100 nanoseconds, and the prefetcher cannot guess the next address.
