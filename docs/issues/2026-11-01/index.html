Israel's election leaves neither side close to a majority

With every vote counted, Netanyahu's bloc has 53 of 120 seats and the opposition bloc led by Gadi Eisenkot has 48, so the Arab parties' 19 seats hold the key.

The final count, as reported by the Times of Israel, gives Likud 27 seats, Eisenkot's Yashar party 21, and the Arab parties (the Joint List and Ra'am) a record 19 between them. Netanyahu's bloc totals 53 seats, down from the 68 the current coalition held, and the anti-Netanyahu bloc has 48. A government needs 61. Turnout was 77.9 percent, the highest in decades.

The arithmetic is awkward. The Arab parties' seats would give the opposition a majority on paper, but every Zionist party except The Democrats has ruled out working with Ra'am or the Joint List. President Isaac Herzog will meet party leaders from Wednesday, once the Central Elections Committee hands him the official results, and has urged them to avoid another election. The new Knesset is sworn in on November 10, and the Times of Israel says coalition talks could run more than two months. Netanyahu and Eisenkot have each said they intend to form the next government.

Investors start asking whether the AI spending boom will pay off

Four tech giants plan over 740 billion dollars of spending this year, and several now burn more cash than they make.

What happened
TechCrunch reports that Alphabet, Amazon, Meta and Microsoft plan more than 740 billion dollars of capital spending this year. Alphabet had negative free cash flow of 9.1 billion dollars in its third quarter and Amazon negative 42.4 billion over twelve months. Microsoft was the exception, with 18.6 billion positive. The five biggest spenders raised a combined 326 billion dollars in debt in 2026, about triple last year, per S&P Global Market Intelligence. Separately, the Financial Times reported that Blackstone told investors Anthropic's debt could get a high-yield, or junk, rating after its planned IPO.
What it means, in plain words
Building AI means buying huge numbers of chips and building data centers before the money comes in, like building a factory before you have customers. Free cash flow is the cash left after paying for that. When it goes negative, companies borrow, and lenders start to care whether the customers will show up.
Why it matters and how it could be used
Nvidia and the whole networking and inference world depend on this spending. If it slows, demand for the hardware XLIO's customers buy could change. If it continues, efficiency work like faster networking becomes more valuable because every wasted cycle costs real money.

Jargon, explained

Capex
Capital expenditure: money spent on long-lived things like data centers and chips.
Free cash flow
Cash a company has left after running the business and paying for investments.
Junk rating
A credit rating saying a borrower is riskier, so lenders charge more interest.

Mistral publishes the weights of a trillion-parameter model

Mistral Large 4 is free to download and aimed at cybersecurity work that companies want to run on their own machines.

What happened
Mistral released the weights of Mistral Large 4, a model with about one trillion parameters, on Hugging Face and through its API, according to TechCrunch. Mistral claims it performs comparably to the Chinese open model GLM-5.3, especially on cyber tasks, while using 47 percent fewer tokens per task, and that it tops open models on Artificial Analysis' Coding Agent Index. TechCrunch gives no independent benchmarks, license terms or architecture details.
What it means, in plain words
Parameters are the numbers a model learned in training, roughly its memory of everything it was taught. Publishing the weights means anyone can download those numbers and run the model themselves, instead of sending data to someone else's servers. Using fewer tokens per task means it needs less text to reach an answer, which makes it cheaper to run.
Why it matters and how it could be used
A bank or government that cannot send sensitive security data to an outside service could run this model inside its own building. A model this size needs many GPUs, so it is also a serving and networking challenge. All performance numbers here are Mistral's own claims.

Jargon, explained

Open weights
The trained numbers are published so anyone can run the model themselves.
Token
A small chunk of text, often a word or part of one, that a model reads and writes.
On-premises
Run on the customer's own computers rather than in someone else's cloud.

NVIDIA publishes a tested recipe for running Dynamo on Kubernetes

A pinned, validated set of software versions for serving AI models at data center scale.

What happened
NVIDIA's technical blog describes a validated Kubernetes runtime for Dynamo, its inference serving software, packaged as recipes in the NVIDIA AI Cluster Runtime. Each recipe pins versions, for example Dynamo Platform 1.4.2 on Amazon EKS with GB200 and GB300 NVL72 racks, and includes validation checks. The post says routing uses KV-cache state reported by the workers, and that Dynamo 1.5 is expected to swap a Go bridge for a native Rust one to reduce per-request overhead. NVIDIA gives no performance numbers in the post.
What it means, in plain words
Dynamo decides which GPU should handle each chat request. Kubernetes is the system that starts and watches programs across many servers. The hard part is getting a dozen pieces of software to work together, so NVIDIA is shipping a known-good combination. The KV cache is the model's short-term memory of the conversation so far. Sending a follow-up to the GPU that already holds it saves recomputing it.
Why it matters and how it could be used
A team setting up an inference cluster can start from a tested recipe instead of a weeks-long integration. It also shows where the effort in AI serving is going: scheduling, routing and moving cache data between machines, which is network-heavy.

Jargon, explained

Kubernetes
Software that starts, stops and monitors programs across many servers.
KV cache
The model's saved working memory of the text so far, reused so it isn't recomputed.
Inference
Running a trained model to answer requests, as opposed to training it.

Google's first AI data center satellite is working in orbit

A small prototype with Google chips is running an AI model in space.

What happened
TechCrunch reports Google's Suncatcher prototype launched earlier this month on a SpaceX Falcon 9 and, after about three weeks of commissioning, is operating nominally, a Google spokesperson said. It carries four TPUs but can run only one at a time, each for about 20 minutes, on up to one kilowatt of solar power. Gemma 4 is preloaded and wrote a haiku in its first test. Two more satellites are planned for 2027, and Google targets the 2030s for the economics to work.
What it means, in plain words
The idea is to put computers where sunlight is constant and cooling is by radiating heat to space. This one is a tiny test. A real data center uses megawatts, so this is roughly a lightbulb compared to a power plant.
Why it matters and how it could be used
The test studies heat and radiation, which can flip bits and corrupt data. Google's ground tests suggested radiation is a minor issue for inference but more of a problem for training. It is far from practical use today.

Jargon, explained

TPU
Tensor Processing Unit: Google's own AI chip.
Bit flip
A stored 0 turning into a 1, or the reverse, often caused by radiation.

What a socket is, and what really happens when an app calls send() and recv()

Sunday track: XLIO and kernel bypass

The big idea: A socket is a handle the operating system gives your program for a network conversation. Calling send() hands your bytes to the kernel, which does the real work of delivering them, and recv() collects what the kernel has already received for you.

First, the basics

Your program cannot touch the network card directly. The operating system kernel owns the hardware and sits between your program and the card. Your program asks the kernel for services through system calls, which are controlled doors into the kernel. Crossing a door costs a little time.

A socket is what you get back when you ask the kernel for a network endpoint with socket(). It is a small integer, a file descriptor, that you pass back to later calls. Behind that number the kernel keeps buffers: a send buffer holding bytes waiting to go out, and a receive buffer holding bytes that arrived but your program has not read yet. TCP, the protocol most sockets use, also needs to be set up first: connect() on the client and listen() and accept() on the server.

How it works, step by step
  1. The app calls send(fd, data, len). The CPU switches from user mode to kernel mode.
  2. The kernel copies the bytes from the app's memory into a socket buffer in kernel memory.
  3. The TCP and IP code in the kernel splits the data into packets, adds headers and picks a route.
  4. The kernel gives the packets to the network card driver, which tells the card to fetch them from memory and transmit them. send() usually returns as soon as the copy is done, not when the data arrives.
  5. On the receiving machine, the card writes arriving packets to memory and signals the kernel, which runs TCP, checks order, and places the bytes in that socket's receive buffer.
  6. The app calls recv(). If data is waiting, the kernel copies it into the app's buffer and returns. If not, the app sleeps until data arrives, or recv() returns immediately in non-blocking mode.
A practical example
A trading app sends a 100-byte order with send(). Those 100 bytes cross into the kernel, are copied once, wrapped in TCP and IP headers, and passed to the card. On the exchange's server the kernel receives, reassembles and queues them until the matching app calls recv(). Each stage adds time, and for a tiny message that overhead is far larger than the time to move 100 bytes.
Why it matters for XLIO
XLIO keeps the socket API your app already uses, but does the buffering, TCP work and card communication in user space, skipping the system call door and the kernel copies.
Key numbers
Typical Ethernet MTU (largest normal packet)1500 bytes
TCP plus IPv4 headers without options40 bytes
Common misconception
A common misconception: send() returning means the data reached the other side. It only means the kernel accepted the bytes into its send buffer. Delivery and acknowledgement happen later, and a send() can even accept fewer bytes than you asked for.

Jargon, explained

System call
A controlled request from a program to the operating system kernel.
File descriptor
A small number that stands for an open resource, such as a socket.
Kernel
The core of the operating system, which owns the hardware.
Socket buffer
Kernel memory holding bytes waiting to be sent or read.
Self-test: If send() returns 100, has the other machine received the 100 bytes?

Not necessarily. It means the kernel copied 100 bytes into the send buffer. They may still be waiting to be sent, in flight, or lost and needing retransmission. Only the TCP acknowledgement from the other side tells the kernel they arrived, and even that does not mean the other app has read them.
